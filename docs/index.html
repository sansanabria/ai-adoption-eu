Using AI safely at work
The adoption numbers above are only half the story — a short internal guide on how to use these tools without leaking anything you shouldn't — view the full slide deck →
AI model ("LLM")
The engine behind chat tools. Trained on huge amounts of text to guess a good next answer — it doesn't know things the way a person does.
Prompt
Just what you type in. A better prompt gets a better answer.
Training data
Everything a model has learned from. On some accounts, what you type today can shape tomorrow's version of the model.
AI hallucination
When it states something false with total confidence — a rule, a source, that simply doesn't exist. Always check anything factual before you rely on it.

Examples of what not to share
Take out the who, keep the what — names, account numbers and exact amounts come out; the actual problem stays in
NEVER PASTE THIS
Client: Jane Doe, Acme Holdings Ltd Account: 88-4471-02 Amount: EUR 41,220, due 14 Mar Login: j.doe / ••••••••
SHARE THIS INSTEAD
"A client's payment of roughly EUR 40k is 2 weeks overdue. What's a firm but professional follow-up email?"
Names a person, a company, an account number, an amount, and a login — once it's sent, it can't be pulled back. Take out the who, keep the what.
Client & personal data
Names, employers, contact details — anyone identifiable, colleague or client.
Account & case numbers
Account IDs, case references, policy numbers — anything that maps back to a specific record.
Exact financial figures
Real balances, amounts, dates or valuations — round them or describe them instead.
Credentials & internal info
Logins, passwords, API keys, and anything not yet public (deals, strategy, unreleased results).
Why AI makes things up (and how to reduce it)
This matters most with an outside tool — there's no one behind it checking your specific answer
No access to our systems
It only knows what you tell it in the conversation.
Knowledge cutoff
A new rule or updated circular might not be in its training data yet.
Built to sound fluent
Not to flag doubt — the tone is the same whether it's sure or just guessing.
Agrees too easily
Disagreeing with it often makes it agree with you, which isn't the same as you being right.
Reduce it by asking for it: "Always tell me where this comes from. If you don't have a real source, or you're not sure, say so instead of guessing."
